> **Note on the "Clean Almond Fruit" Boundary:** The system boundary is defined around **clean, debris-free almond fruit** as it enters the hulling and shelling lines after precleaning. Defining the parent input on a clean-fruit basis keeps the reported yields independent of regional and seasonal variation in orchard debris.

*Note on almond varieties:* California grows many almond varieties, with Nonpareil the dominant variety (~40% of acreage) [^4^][^11^]. Varieties differ in their kernel-to-shell-to-hull ratio: Nonpareil and other modern soft-shell varieties yield ~28–33% kernels with thinner shells, while older hard-shell varieties (e.g., Padre, Butte) yield ~20–24% kernels with more shell mass. The 31% kernel yield used in this document reflects the composition of newer plantings rather than the current standing-orchard average. New orchards are being established predominantly with higher-yielding soft-shell varieties, and older hard-shell blocks are progressively being replaced, so this value is the more representative basis for the forward-looking assessment the parent manuscript supports.

## 3. Almond Hulling Processing System

### 3.1 Process Description
Almond hulling is a single-stage mechanical process that separates clean, in-hull almonds into three co-products: almond kernels, almond hulls, and almond shells. The process is physically straightforward — the hull is removed from the in-shell almond, then the shell is cracked to release the kernel — and is modeled as a single stage because the manuscript requires one allocation across the three final streams. In-shell almonds are traded in some export markets, but hulling and shelling are treated here as a single integrated operation.

**Processing steps (within system boundary):**
- **Hulling:** The clean almonds pass through a hulling machine (typically a shear roll or impact huller) that cracks and removes the hull.
- **Shelling:** The in-shell almonds pass through a cracking machine that cracks the shells. Air-leg separators separate shells from kernels based on density.
- **Kernel cleaning and sorting:** Electronic color sorters remove defective kernels and shell fragments.
- **Sizing & Packaging:** Kernels are sized and packaged for shipment.

*(Note: Precleaning to remove field debris occurs upstream of this system boundary.)*

*Rounding note:* The exact mass allocation percentages are Kernels 32.17%, Hulls 48.17%, and Shells 19.66%. Rounded to one decimal place these sum to 100.1%; the unrounded values sum to 100.0%.

### 7.2 Basis for the input moisture value
The 8.45% input moisture is the yield-weighted average of the three co-product moisture contents: kernels (95% DM), hulls (90% DM) and shells (90% DM). Hulling and shelling are purely mechanical separations, with no chemical transformation or artificial drying inside the system boundary, so the moisture leaving the process is the moisture that entered it.

This value differs from the generic 93% DM (7% moisture) figure sometimes quoted for field-run almond loads. Field-run deliveries include external debris removed during precleaning, upstream of this boundary, and their moisture varies with harvest timing and field conditions. Because the input moisture here is derived from the outputs, the mass balance in Section 7.1 closes by construction and should be read as a statement of the basis used, not as an independent verification of the yields.

## 9. Data Quality and Limitations

### 9.1 High-Confidence Data
- **Kernel DM% (95%):** High. Standard for almond kernels at harvest.
- **Kernel price ($4,800/t):** High. Supported by USDA NASS 2024 data [^4^].

### 9.2 Medium-Confidence Data
- **Yields (0.31, 0.49, 0.20):** Medium. Composition data from the Almond Board of California [^5^]; the 31% kernel fraction reflects newer soft-shell plantings rather than the current standing-orchard average (see Section 1.2).
- **Hull DM% (90%) & Shell DM% (90%):** Medium. Plausible but represents specific late-harvest conditions.
- **Shell price ($40/t):** Medium. Unverifiable. Shells are often given away or used for biomass.

### 9.3 Known Limitations
- **Extreme allocation divergence:** The 64.4 pp divergence between economic and mass allocation for kernels means the choice of method drastically impacts LCA results. Sensitivity analysis is highly recommended.
- **Kernel price volatility:** Almond prices are highly volatile. Because kernels dominate economic allocation (96.6%), price volatility directly translates into allocation volatility.
- **Hull value and emerging markets:** If hull values increase substantially (e.g., $500/t for food ingredients), the economic allocation would shift significantly away from kernels (dropping to ~85.5%).
- **Variety-dependent yields:** Kernel yield varies by variety, from ~20–24% for older hard-shell types to ~28–33% for Nonpareil and other soft-shell varieties. The 31% used here reflects newer plantings; an assessment of the current standing-orchard average would use a lower kernel yield and a correspondingly higher shell fraction.
- **Upstream Precleaning Boundary:** This model sets the boundary at the clean fruit to ensure strict mass balance closure. If modeling from the orchard gate, practitioners must apply a 5–13% mass loss factor to account for precleaning debris and allocate precleaning energy to the primary fruit input.
- **Component Moisture Variability:** The 8.45% input moisture is a weighted average. Actual load moisture will vary based on harvest timing and field conditions, but the mass balance will always close perfectly relative to the specific component moistures measured at the huller.
