> **Note on "field-run almonds" definition:** "Field-run almonds" (also called "in-hull almonds" or "as-harvested almonds") refers to the whole almond product as delivered from the orchard to the huller/sheller facility. This includes the kernel (nut meat), the shell (endocarp), the hull (mesocarp and exocarp), and any field debris (dirt, twigs, leaves). The EPA AP-42 document for almond processing reports that field-run almonds typically contain ~13% debris in addition to the kernel, shell, and hull fractions [^3^]. This document's yield data (31% kernels, 49% hulls, 20% shells = 100%) is on a "fruit weight" (debris-free) basis, as reported by the Almond Board of California [^5^], meaning the debris has been removed during precleaning before the yield fractions are measured. The DM balance gap (Section 7) is consistent with a small debris fraction.

> **Note on almond varieties:** California grows many almond varieties, with Nonpareil being the dominant variety (~40% of acreage) [^4^][^13^]. Different varieties have different kernel-to-shell-to-hull ratios: Nonpareil has a higher kernel yield (~28–33%) and thinner shells, while hard-shell varieties (e.g., Padre, Butte) have lower kernel yields (~20–24%) and more shell mass. The yields in this document represent industry averages across all varieties as reported by the Almond Board of California [^5^].

## 3. Almond Hulling Processing System

### 3.1 Process Description

Almond hulling is a single-stage mechanical process that separates field-run almonds (in-hull) into three co-products: almond kernels, almond hulls, and almond shells. The process is physically straightforward — the hull is removed from the in-shell almond, then the shell is cracked to release the kernel — but is modeled as a single stage because all three co-products are produced from a single integrated operation with no intermediate product that can be sold or diverted to a separate pathway.

**Processing steps:**

1. **Receiving and precleaning:** Field-run almonds are received at the huller/sheller facility and passed through a precleaning system to remove field debris (dirt, stones, twigs, leaves). This debris typically constitutes 5–13% of the field-run weight [^3^].
2. **Hulling:** The almonds pass through a hulling machine (typically a shear roll or impact huller) that cracks and removes the hull from the in-shell almond. The hulls are separated by air aspiration and screening.
3. **Shelling:** The in-shell almonds (kernels still inside shells) pass through a cracking machine (typically a roll cracker or shear roll) that cracks the shells. The cracked material passes over a vibrating screen or through an air-leg separator that separates the shells from the kernels based on density differences.
4. **Kernel cleaning and sorting:** The separated kernels pass through additional cleaning steps to remove remaining shell fragments, doubles, and foreign material. Electronic color sorters (optical sorters) remove defective kernels, insect damage, and shell fragments.
5. **Sizing (optional):** Kernels may be sized by passing over perforated screens to separate different kernel size grades. Sizing is a grading step, not a processing step, and does not affect the allocation.
6. **Packaging and storage:** Kernels are packaged in bins, tote bags, or cartons for shipment. Kernels are stored under controlled conditions (cool, dry) to maintain quality.

**Co-products:**

- **Almond kernels (nut meats):** The primary product — the edible seed (kernel) inside the shell. Almond kernels are a high-value food product consumed as snacks, in baking, as almond butter, almond flour, almond milk, and as an ingredient in various food products. Kernels are graded by size, variety, and quality, with Nonpareil large kernels commanding the highest prices.
- **Almond hulls:** The fibrous outer layer (mesocarp and exocarp) that surrounds the shell. Hulls are a significant by-product used primarily as dairy cattle feed in California's Central Valley. Hulls contain sugars (sucrose and reducing sugars), fiber, and minerals, making them a palatable and nutritious feed supplement. Hulls can also be used as soil amendment, bioenergy feedstock, or (emerging) as a food ingredient.
- **Almond shells:** The hard, woody inner layer (endocarp) that encloses the kernel. Shells are the lowest-value co-product, used as livestock bedding, boiler fuel, soil amendment (as a mulch or biochar precursor), or in some cases as a feed ingredient mixed with hulls. Shells are high in lignin and have limited nutritional value for livestock.

> **Note on process integration:** Almond hulling and shelling is modeled as a single stage because the hulling and shelling steps are integrated within a single facility and there is no market for the intermediate product (in-shell almonds after hull removal but before shell cracking). While in-shell almonds are sometimes sold (particularly for hard-shell varieties), this is a separate market and not the standard processing pathway for California almonds, which are predominantly soft-shell varieties intended for kernel production.

## 4. Co-Product Yields and Properties

### 4.1 Processing Yields

| Co-product | Yield (t/t field-run almonds) | Range | Source & Calculation |
|------------|------------------------------|-------|---------------------|
| **Almond kernels** | 0.31 | 0.27–0.35 | Midpoint of range. Almond Board of California [^5^] reports kernels as 31% of total fruit weight for the 2024/2025 crop year. The 0.31 value is the mathematical midpoint of the stated range: (0.27 + 0.35) / 2 = 0.31. Kernel yield depends primarily on variety: Nonpareil and other soft-shell varieties yield ~28–33% kernels, while hard-shell varieties (Padre, Butte, Mission) yield ~20–24% kernels. Growing conditions (irrigation, soil quality) and harvest timing also affect kernel development and yield. |
| **Almond hulls** | 0.49 | 0.45–0.53 | Midpoint of range. Almond Board of California [^5^] reports hulls as 49% of total fruit weight. The 0.49 value is the mathematical midpoint of the stated range: (0.45 + 0.53) / 2 = 0.49. Hull yield depends on variety (soft-shell varieties tend to have more hull, hard-shell varieties less), irrigation (well-watered trees produce larger, thicker hulls), and harvest timing (earlier harvest = higher hull moisture and weight). |
| **Almond shells** | 0.20 | 0.16–0.24 | Midpoint of range. Almond Board of California [^5^] reports shells as 20% of total fruit weight. The 0.20 value is the mathematical midpoint of the stated range: (0.16 + 0.24) / 2 = 0.20. Shell yield is inversely related to kernel yield — hard-shell varieties have more shell and less kernel, while soft-shell varieties have less shell and more kernel. The shell fraction also depends on shell thickness, which varies significantly by variety. |

> **Note on yield relationships:** Kernel, hull, and shell yields sum to 1.00 (100%), meaning every kilogram of field-run almonds (excluding debris) is accounted for as one of the three co-products. This is a key feature of almond hulling: there are no non-marketed mass streams other than the small debris fraction removed during precleaning. The three yields are constrained to sum to 1.00, so an increase in one component must be offset by a decrease in another. The primary trade-off is between kernel yield and shell yield (inversely correlated across varieties), while hull yield is relatively stable.

> **Note on EPA AP-42 comparison and yield reconciliation:** The EPA AP-42 document for almond processing [^3^] reports a different yield distribution: "On average, field weight yields 13 percent debris, 50 percent hulls, 14 percent shells, and 23 percent clean almond meats and pieces." This differs from the Almond Board of California data (31% kernels, 49% hulls, 20% shells) used in this document. The key differences are: (a) the AP-42 data includes a 13% debris fraction on a "field weight" basis, while the ABC data is on a "fruit weight" (debris-free) basis, (b) the AP-42 shell yield (14%) is lower than the ABC's 20%, and (c) the AP-42 kernel yield (23%) is lower than the ABC's 31%. Even recalculating the AP-42 data on a debris-free basis yields 26.4% kernels, 57.5% hulls, and 16.1% shells, which still differs from the ABC data. These differences likely reflect: (1) the AP-42 data dates from 1995 and reflects older, lower-yielding varieties, while the ABC data reflects modern California varieties with higher kernel crack-out ratios; (2) different variety mixes (modern California production is dominated by Nonpareil and other soft-shell varieties with higher kernel yields); and (3) different sampling and measurement approaches. This document uses the ABC data as the primary source because it is more recent, from the industry's own authoritative body, and reflects modern California almond production conditions.

### 4.2 Dry Matter Contents

| Co-product | DM (%) | Basis |
|------------|--------|-------|
| Almond kernels | 95.0% | Almond kernels at harvest typically contain 4–6% moisture. The USDA standard for shelled almonds specifies maximum moisture for grading purposes [^8^]. Kernels are further dried during storage and processing to 3–4% moisture for long-term storage, but the 95% DM value represents kernels at the point of hulling/shelling, before any additional drying. The 95.0% value is the midpoint of the 4–6% moisture range (94–96% DM). |
| Almond hulls | 90.0% | Almond hulls at harvest typically contain 8–20% moisture [^5^]. A comprehensive review in *Foods* (PMC 11941897) reports almond hulls have 8–20% moisture, with the wider range reflecting variability in harvest timing and field conditions. The 90% DM value (10% moisture) represents hulls as they emerge from the hulling machine [^6^][^7^], which corresponds to the lower end of the moisture range (late-harvested, well-dried conditions). Early-harvested almonds have higher hull moisture (85–88% DM). The 90% DM value is an assumption representing typical processing conditions and should be treated as an estimate. |
| Almond shells | 90.0% | Almond shells at harvest typically contain ~8–12% moisture, though this range is based on limited data and cannot be independently verified from authoritative sources. The 90% DM value (10% moisture) represents shells as they emerge from the shelling machine [^6^][^7^]. **This value is estimated and unverifiable** — no authoritative source was found that provides almond shell DM data. Shells are dense, woody structures that dry quickly on the tree. |

## 6. Allocation Methodology

### 6.1 Economic Allocation

| Co-product | Calculation | Allocation |
|------------|-------------|------------|
| Almond kernels | (1,488.00 ÷ 1,540.10) × 100 | **96.6%** |
| Almond hulls | (44.10 ÷ 1,540.10) × 100 | **2.9%** |
| Almond shells | (8.00 ÷ 1,540.10) × 100 | **0.5%** |
| **Total** | | **100.0%** |

### 6.2 Mass Allocation

| Co-product | Calculation | Allocation |
|------------|-------------|------------|
| Almond kernels | (0.2945 ÷ 0.9155) × 100 | **32.2%** |
| Almond hulls | (0.4410 ÷ 0.9155) × 100 | **48.2%** |
| Almond shells | (0.1800 ÷ 0.9155) × 100 | **19.7%** |
| **Total** | | **100.1%** |

> **Note on mass allocation rounding:** The exact mass allocation percentages are Kernels 32.16%, Hulls 48.17%, and Shells 19.66%, which sum to 99.99% (effectively 100.0%). When rounded to one decimal place, these become 32.2%, 48.2%, and 19.7%, summing to 100.1%. The 0.1% excess is a rounding artifact; the true values sum to 100.0%. These values were calculated using exact DM outputs (0.2945, 0.4410, 0.1800; total 0.9155) without intermediate rounding, consistent with the protocol described in Section 4.3.

### 6.3 Comparison

| Co-product | Economic | Mass | Difference |
|------------|----------|------|------------|
| Almond kernels | 96.6% | 32.2% | **+64.4 pp** |
| Almond hulls | 2.9% | 48.2% | **-45.3 pp** |
| Almond shells | 0.5% | 19.7% | **-19.1 pp** |

The **64.4 pp divergence for almond kernels** is by far the largest economic-vs-mass allocation difference of any crop in this review. For comparison, the next largest divergences are: safflower oil (+43.2 pp), citrus juice (+43.1 pp), and cotton lint (+34.6 pp in cascade). The almond kernel divergence exceeds even these because almond kernels combine three extreme characteristics: (a) very high price ($4,800/t, far exceeding any other primary product in this review), (b) relatively low DM share (32.2%, because hulls and shells carry the majority of the DM), and (c) moderate yield (0.31 t/t, meaning most of the input mass goes to by-products).

Under economic allocation, almond kernels bear 96.6% of the environmental burden despite representing only 32.2% of the DM output. Under mass allocation, kernels bear only 32.2% of the burden, with hulls receiving the largest share (48.2%). This 64 pp gap means that the choice between economic and mass allocation has a far greater impact on the LCA results for almond kernels than for any other crop in this review.

The practical implication is significant: an LCA of almond kernels using economic allocation would assign ~3.0× more environmental burden per tonne of DM than one using mass allocation (96.6 ÷ 32.2 = 3.00). This divergence warrants careful consideration in LCA studies of almond products, and results should be presented using both methods with a clear explanation of the implications.

> **Balance assessment:** The DM output is 0.0145 t (1.6%) below the DM input. This deficit represents field debris (dirt, twigs, leaf fragments) removed during precleaning, which the EPA AP-42 [^3^] reports as approximately 5–13% of field-run weight. The 1.6% DM gap is well within the acceptable range and is consistent with a debris fraction of approximately 1.5–2% of the field-run weight (after initial field cleaning by the harvester). The as-is mass balance closes exactly (kernels + hulls + shells = 1.000 t), confirming that the yield data is internally consistent and the gap is entirely a DM balance issue related to the debris fraction.

> **Note on input DM derivation:** The stated input DM of ~93% (~7% moisture) is an industry estimate [^1^][^2^]. The weighted average DM calculated from the component DM values and yields in this document is: (0.31 × 95% + 0.49 × 90% + 0.20 × 90%) / (0.31 + 0.49 + 0.20) = 91.55%. The 1.45 percentage point difference between the estimated input DM (93%) and the derived weighted average (91.55%) is consistent with the presence of debris (which would increase the overall DM if debris has lower moisture than the fruit components) and/or variation in component moisture contents under field conditions. The choice of input DM does not affect the allocation calculations, which depend only on co-product DM outputs and prices.

> **Note on mass balance closure:** The as-is mass balance closes exactly at 1.000 t, meaning the three co-products account for all of the input mass on an as-is basis. This is a distinctive feature of almond hulling compared to most other crops in this review, where significant water is removed during processing (e.g., sugarcane, sugar beet, citrus). Almond hulling is a purely mechanical separation process — no water is added or removed, and no chemical reactions occur. The three output streams are simply the physical components of the input, separated by mechanical means. This makes the as-is mass balance trivially exact, but the DM balance is more informative because it accounts for the different moisture contents of the three streams and the debris fraction.

### 9.3 Known Limitations

1. **Extreme allocation divergence:** The 64.4 pp divergence between economic allocation (96.6%) and mass allocation (32.2%) for almond kernels is the largest of any crop in this review. This means that the choice of allocation method has a far greater impact on the LCA results for almonds than for any other crop. Under economic allocation, the environmental burden per tonne of almond kernels is ~3.0× higher than under mass allocation. LCA practitioners should present results using both methods and conduct sensitivity analysis on the allocation choice, as recommended by ISO 14044 for cases where allocation significantly affects study conclusions.

2. **Kernel price volatility:** Almond kernel prices are among the most volatile of any US agricultural commodity. The 5-year average (2020–2024) was approximately $1.76/lb ($3,876/t), while the 2024 specific average was $2.14/lb ($4,714/t). The $4,800/t midpoint represents the 2024–2025 period, but prices have ranged from ~$1.50/lb ($3,300/t) in 2020 to ~$2.50/lb ($5,500/t) in 2022–2023. Because kernels dominate the economic allocation (96.6%), price volatility directly translates into allocation volatility — a 25% change in kernel price would shift the kernel economic allocation by approximately 0.7–1.1 pp.

3. **Hull value and emerging markets:** Almond hulls have traditionally been sold as dairy cattle feed at $60–120/t, but emerging applications could significantly increase their value: (a) almond hull flour as a food ingredient (potentially $500–1,000/t), (b) bioenergy feedstock, and (c) extraction of bioactive compounds (antioxidants, phenolics). If hull values increase substantially, the economic allocation would shift significantly away from kernels toward hulls. For example, if hulls were valued at $500/t, the kernel economic allocation would drop from 96.6% to approximately 85.5% (calculated as: 1,488 / (1,488 + 245 + 8) = 85.5%), a change of 11.1 pp.

4. **Shell disposal and negative pricing:** In some regions and market conditions, almond shells have effectively zero or negative value — the huller/sheller must pay for disposal or transportation. Using a zero price for shells would increase the kernel economic allocation from 96.6% to 97.1% (a change of +0.5 pp), calculated as: 1,488 / (1,488 + 44.10 + 0) = 97.1%. This is a modest change because shells contribute only 0.5% of revenue at $40/t. Using a negative price (disposal cost) would further increase the kernel allocation, but the effect remains small.

5. **Variety-dependent yields:** The yield data in this document represents industry averages across all California almond varieties, as reported by the Almond Board of California [^5^]. However, variety-specific yields can differ substantially from the averages: Nonpareil typically yields ~28–33% kernels, ~46–52% hulls, and ~17–22% shells, while hard-shell varieties (Padre, Butte) yield ~20–24% kernels, ~48–52% hulls, and ~26–30% shells. These differences affect both mass and economic allocations. Studies focused on specific varieties should use variety-specific yield data.

6. **Field debris and the AP-42 discrepancy:** The EPA AP-42 document [^3^] reports that field-run almonds contain ~13% debris, which is significantly higher than the ~1.5–2% debris implied by the 1.6% DM gap in this document. The difference likely reflects improvements in harvesting technology (modern sweepers and pickers remove more debris in the field) and/or different definitions of "field-run." The Almond Board of California's yield data (31/49/20) is on a "fruit weight" basis that excludes debris. If the actual debris fraction is higher than 2%, the yield data in this table may overstate the co-product yields relative to a true "as-delivered-from-orchard" basis, and the true input mass should include the debris fraction.

7. **Geographic concentration:** Virtually all US almond production is concentrated in California's Central Valley. This means that regional price variation (a significant factor for crops like sugarcane and sugar beet) is less of an issue for almonds. However, local market conditions within the Central Valley can still affect hull and shell prices — proximity to dairies (for hulls) and biomass facilities (for shells) significantly affects the prices that huller/shellers can obtain.

8. **Organic vs. conventional production:** The organic almond price premium at the farm gate is significantly smaller than the USDA RMA insurance price election ratio suggests. The USDA RMA price elections are $2.00/lb for conventional and $3.00/lb for organic [^12^], a ratio of 1.50 (50%). However, this is an insurance construct, not a market price premium. UC Davis 2024 Sample Costs data shows organic almond prices at approximately $2.25/lb vs. conventional $2.14/lb, a premium of only ~5%. The actual farm-gate organic premium is typically 5–15%, not 50%. If organic production is being modeled, prices of approximately $5,280–5,520/t for organic kernels (5–15% premium over the $4,800/t conventional price) should be used, not $7,200/t (which would imply a 50% premium). Organic hulls and shells may also command premiums in organic dairy and livestock markets.

9. **Unverifiable data — shells:** Almond shell moisture content, DM%, and market price per tonne cannot be independently verified from authoritative sources. The values used in this document (90% DM, $40/t) are estimates based on industry knowledge and analogy to similar feedstocks. These should be flagged as uncertain in any LCA study, and sensitivity analysis should be conducted on these parameters.

10. **Derived input moisture:** The ~7% moisture (~93% DM) figure for field-run almonds is an industry estimate, not a directly measured value from a single authoritative source. The weighted average calculated from the component DM values and yields in this document is 91.55% DM (~8.45% moisture). The 1.45 percentage point difference is attributed to debris and field conditions but cannot be precisely verified.
